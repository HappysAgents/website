# Research Report: Planned.llc Business Model & US Home Maintenance Subscription Market

**Researcher:** Happy (Chief of Staff)
**Date:** 2026-03-05
**Status:** Draft — writing incrementally as research progresses

---

## TL;DR

- **Planned.llc is a lean, local-feeling quarterly home maintenance subscription** — HVAC, gutters, water heaters, drains, dryer vents, quarterly walkthroughs + photo reports. Pricing not published (call/text to start). No funding found; appears bootstrapped and small.
- **The broader category is validated by VC-backed comps:** Honey Homes ($21M raised, $250–$395/month, employee model, Bay Area + DFW + LA) and Super (home warranty + maintenance hybrid, ~$133/month) both show real demand, but the market is still fragmented and no clear national winner.
- **Unit economics are brutal at small scale:** Labor-heavy, geographic constraint, high churn risk if service quality wavers. The model only works if subscription revenue reliably covers fully-loaded labor + materials + scheduling overhead, which requires 150–200+ homes per technician route area.

## Section 1: What Exactly Does Planned.llc Offer?

**Services included (from website copy):**
- HVAC servicing (pre-season tune-ups)
- Furnace filter replacements
- Water heater flushes
- Drain care
- Appliance maintenance
- Gutter and downspout cleaning

**What this looks like operationally:**

**What appears to be EXCLUDED:**

- Electrical work
- Landscaping

**CTA is call/text — no online booking or pricing page.** This is characteristic of a small, local, or early-stage operation. It limits scalability but may work for building trust in a local market.

**DATA THIN — Planned.llc does not publish pricing publicly.**

The website CTA is "Call or text to get started." No pricing tiers, no mention of monthly vs. annual, no cost listed.

**What can be inferred:**
- Quarterly visit model (4x/year) likely maps to a flat monthly or annual fee
- Comparable regional services in the US price quarterly-only plans at $99–$199/month (~$1,200–$2,400/year)
- Honey Homes (premium, dedicated handyman, monthly visits) charges $250–$395/month as a benchmark

**No tiers are visible.** Single product offering (Quarterly Home Maintenance Plan).

**Why no public pricing matters:** Either (a) they're small and price case-by-case, or (b) they're testing price sensitivity. Either way, it's a friction point for conversion.

## Section 3: Business Model — Employees vs. Contractors, Staffing

**DATA THIN — No employment model disclosed by Planned.llc.**

**What the website signals:**
- Language like "we show up and show up ready" suggests in-house staff or at least curated crew
- Photo reports and consistent quarterly scheduling implies reliability focus — contractors are harder to standardize
- No mentions of a marketplace or matching platform

**Industry context for comparisons:**
- **Honey Homes (closest comp):** Explicitly employee model. Salaried handypeople with benefits (parental leave, PTO). Rationale: consistency, quality control, brand trust. This is the high-cost, high-quality approach.
- **Angi/Thumbtack/TaskRabbit:** Marketplace/contractor models. Lower cost but variable quality.
- **Most small regional services:** W-2 employees for core staff, 1099 contractors for surge or specialty.

**Planned.llc most likely operates with:**
- Small team of W-2 technicians or very trusted contractors
- Possibly 1–2 person operation at current stage
- No tech platform visible (no app mentioned, no scheduling portal)

**Quarterly visit cadence means each technician can serve many more homes per day than a monthly model.** Rough math: 4 homes/day × 5 days × 50 weeks = 1,000 home visits/year = 250 home subscriptions per technician. This is a fundamentally different (and more scalable) cost structure than Honey Homes' monthly model.

## Section 6: Competitor Landscape

### Honey Homes (closest direct comp)
- **Model:** Dedicated salaried handyman, monthly visits, membership
- **Pricing:** $2,000–$4,750/year (multiple tiers); older plans ~$250–$395/month
- **Geography:** SF Bay Area, Dallas-Fort Worth, Los Angeles
- **Funding:** $21.35M total (Khosla Ventures, Pear VC, Era Ventures)
- **Scale:** 1,000+ members (as of late 2023), 50+ handypeople, 75 total employees
- **Differentiation:** Dedicated person (same handyman every visit), app, 30-day guarantee
- **Weakness:** Premium price, limited geography, not quarterly — heavier commitment

### Super (hellosuper.com)
- **Model:** Home warranty + maintenance hybrid subscription
- **Pricing:** ~$133/month (based on customer reports); subscription covers repair AND some maintenance
- **Coverage:** Select US states only
- **Services:** HVAC tune-ups, pest control, dryer vent, lawn care, re-keying, carpet cleaning + warranty coverage for systems/appliances
- **Differentiation:** Bundled warranty + maintenance in one subscription — unique value prop
- **Weakness:** Warranty claims handling is the pain point; customer reviews cite claim denials; higher cost than pure maintenance

### Frontdoor / American Home Shield (AHS)
- **Model:** Traditional home warranty (reactive repair coverage), not maintenance subscription
- **Pricing:** $30–$100+/month depending on plan; service fee per call ($100–$125)
- **Scale:** Public company (NASDAQ: FTDR); national scale; 2M+ customers
- **Revenue:** ~$1.7B annual revenue (2023)
- **Differentiation:** Brand recognition, national contractor network, legal/financial protection frame
- **Weakness:** Pure warranty model — covers breakdowns, NOT preventive maintenance. Different product from Planned.
- **Adjacent:** AHS/Frontdoor has added some maintenance services (HVAC tune-ups via Frontdoor Pro) but core product is reactive

### Angi (formerly Angie's List / HomeAdvisor)
- **Model:** Lead generation marketplace / project matchmaking
- **Revenue model:** Contractor fees + homeowner subscriptions ($29.99/month Angi Key)
- **Scale:** ~22M+ service professionals, publicly traded (NYSE: ANGI)
- **Weakness:** Variable quality, not a recurring maintenance relationship — one-off job matching

### Porch Group
- **Model:** Home services platform / insurance + moving bundling
- **Revenue model:** Data/insurance referrals, not direct maintenance delivery
- **Scale:** Public company; primarily B2B (works with movers, inspectors, agents)
- **Not a direct comp:** Porch is infrastructure/data, not a consumer subscription

### Homestewards (SF Bay Area)

- **Pricing:** Not prominently published; similar positioning to Planned.llc
- **Geography:** SF + North Bay only
- **Similar operator to Planned.llc** — regional, quality-focused, not VC-backed at scale

### Betty Brigade

- **Pricing:** Not published (consult required)
- **Geography:** Local US markets

## Section 7: Unit Economics

**DATA THIN — No public financial data for Planned.llc. The following is modeled from comparable operators.**

### Revenue per customer
- Estimated Planned.llc pricing: ~$99–$149/month = $1,188–$1,788/year per customer
- Using midpoint: ~$1,500/year/customer

### Visit frequency and cost
- 4 visits/year (quarterly)
- Visit duration: ~2–3 hours per home (seasonal checklist takes longer than a simple repair)
- At $75–$95/hour all-in labor cost: $150–$285 per visit
- 4 visits/year × $200 avg = $800/year in direct labor per customer
- Materials/supplies (filters, small parts): ~$50–$100/year per customer
- **Total COGS: ~$850–$900/year per customer**
- **Gross margin per customer: ~$600–$700/year (40–47% gross margin)**

### Technician capacity
- If each quarterly visit takes ~2.5 hours including travel: 3 homes/day/technician
- 5 days/week × 50 weeks = 750 home-visits/year per technician
- At quarterly cadence: 750 / 4 = ~187 homes per technician
- At $1,500/year/customer × 187 = $280,500 revenue per technician
- At $70K fully-loaded labor cost/technician: 25% labor cost ratio per technician
- **Each technician can serve ~150–200 homes profitably**

### Path to profitability
- Breakeven for solo operator (owner + 1 technician + overhead): ~100–150 customers
- 200+ customers: profitable
- 500+ customers: can support 3 technicians + management layer + marketing

### Honey Homes benchmark (public data)
- $21M raised, targeting $10M ARR (2024 target) → implies ~3,300–5,000 members at $250–$395/month
- 50+ handypeople for 1,000+ members → ~20 homes per handyman
- Monthly visits with same person → much lower capacity than quarterly model
- This is the premium, high-labor version — Planned.llc's quarterly model is structurally more capital efficient

### Key risk: Customer concentration
- Small operator with 50–100 customers = extremely fragile. One bad review, one technician quit = existential
- Minimum viable scale is probably 150+ paying customers in a dense geographic area

## Section 8: US Market Size

**Home Services overall:**
- US home services market: $650–$842B (various analyst estimates, 2025–2026)
- Growing at 3–10% CAGR depending on segment
- Repair & maintenance is the largest subsegment

**Homeowner spending data:**
- Average routine maintenance: $2,458/year per household (Angi survey)
- Average emergency repairs: $2,321/year per household (Angi survey)
- Combined avg: ~$4,779/year → this is the stat Planned.llc references ("over $4,000")
- High-end: $4,000–$22,000/year depending on home age, size, location (AHS survey)
- Regular maintenance (not emergency): $1,400–$2,300/year (homekeep.com benchmark)

**Preventive home maintenance subscription specifically:**
- 83M+ owner-occupied housing units in the US (Census)
- If even 1% adopted a $1,500/year subscription: **$1.25B market for maintenance subscriptions alone**
- Full 5% penetration: **$6.2B**
- Subscriptions currently capture a tiny fraction of total homeowner maintenance spend

**Home warranty market (adjacent):**
- US home warranty market: $4.3–$4.6B (2024 estimates, IBISWorld/homemembership.com)
- Growing at ~3.7–6.8% CAGR
- Frontdoor/AHS alone: $1.7B revenue. This is the reactive coverage version.
- Preventive subscription is the logical upgrade/complement to warranty coverage
- Global home warranty market estimate: $10B+ (different analysts, broader scope)

**Why the market is big but fragmented:**
- Homeownership is inherently local (geographic service constraint)
- No national brand owns preventive maintenance subscriptions yet
- Honey Homes is the VC-backed attempt, but only in 3 metro areas after 3+ years
- Most operators are small local businesses with no technology or brand scale

## Section 9: Known Failure Modes & Risks

### 1. Labor economics don't work at small scale
- At <100 customers, revenue doesn't cover technician + overhead + marketing
- Founder often becomes the technician → no leverage, burnout risk
- Hiring good technicians is hard; training them is expensive; retaining them is even harder

### 2. Geographic density problem
- Each technician needs customers clustered in a small geographic area to minimize drive time
- Spreading customers across too wide an area kills margin via wasted travel
- Solution: launch hyperlocal, not metro-wide

### 3. Seasonal demand mismatch
- Quarterly visits concentrate in spring and fall (most in-demand seasons)
- Customer expectations for specific timing ("before winter hits") create scheduling pressure
- Also: spring/fall are when people do most home projects → competition for customer attention

### 4. Churn from perceived low value
- If nothing "goes wrong" in year 1, customer cancels ("I didn't need this")
- Preventive services are invisible — customer doesn't see avoided disasters
- Photo reports help, but the value narrative must be constantly reinforced

### 5. Quality consistency is the whole product
- One bad visit = cancellation + negative review
- One no-show = permanent trust damage
- Employee model (like Honey Homes) reduces this risk but increases labor cost

### 6. Scope creep / underpricing
- "Small adjustments" is vague — customers escalate asks
- Without clear scope boundaries, a quarterly visit can turn into a 5-hour job for a $99/month customer
- Explicit scope limits in the contract are essential

### 7. Competitor entry from adjacent players
- Frontdoor/AHS already moving into maintenance (HVAC tune-ups)
- HVAC companies (Service Champions, etc.) already offer annual maintenance plans
- TaskRabbit/Angi could add subscription layer
- Walmart Home Services has been piloting home services at scale

### 8. Technology/app gap
- Honey Homes built an app for member communication, scheduling, tracking
- Planned.llc (and similar small ops) run on "call or text" → hard to scale, hard to retain

## Section 10: What Makes Planned.llc Interesting/Differentiated?

**Based on available data:**

1. **Simplicity:** One plan, four visits, clear scope. No complexity. Easy to sell, easy to deliver.

2. **Positioning clarity:** Explicit focus on prevention vs. repair. The "$4,000 in avoided repairs" stat is a compelling value prop that's easy for homeowners to internalize.

3. **Photo documentation:** Building accountability and trust through visual records is a smart, low-tech differentiation. Creates proof of value.

4. **Quarterly model = better unit economics than monthly competitors.** 4 visits/year vs. 12 = 3x more homes per technician at same revenue. This is a meaningful structural advantage.

5. **The honest critique:** Planned.llc's website is thin, pricing is opaque, and there are no visible customer reviews. It reads like an early-stage or very local operation. The brand promise is there, but there's no evidence of scale, systems, or technology.

**DATA THIN:** No verified customer count, no verified revenue, no funding history, no reviews on Yelp/Google/BBB found. Geographic location not identified on website. This could be a 10-customer side hustle or a 300-customer local business — impossible to tell from public data.

## Key Takeaways for Greece Adaptation

1. **The quarterly model is the right starting point for Greece.** Monthly visits (Honey Homes model) require dense premium markets (SF/NYC pricing). Quarterly visits are economically viable at earlier scale and map well to seasonal patterns (pre-summer A/C prep, pre-winter boiler check, etc.).

2. **Employee model vs. contractor will be a defining choice.** Greece has a large informal labor economy — finding reliable licensed contractors is a known pain point. A semi-employed model (consistent crew, not marketplace) may be necessary for trust and quality.

3. **The ICP translation:** US ICP = dual-income suburban homeowner. Greece adaptation = (a) Airbnb/short-term rental hosts who fear guest complaints and bad reviews, (b) expat homeowners who lack local trade networks, (c) time-poor Athens professionals. Airbnb hosts may be the strongest ICP — they have financial motivation and already pay for professional services.

4. **Services need adapting:** Greece has no furnace filters. The checklist shifts to: A/C (critical), boiler/heat pump maintenance, water heater, solar hot water panels, cisterns/tanks (on islands), earthquake-related structural checks (marble staircase tiles, walls, balconies), mold/moisture in older buildings, pool maintenance (relevant for villas/islands).

5. **Pricing translation:** US quarterly plan ~$1,200–$2,000/year. Greece pricing likely needs to be €400–€800/year to fit the market — lower than US but still viable if costs (labor, parts) are also lower. Athens professionals/expats may tolerate higher pricing (€600–€1,200/year).

6. **The Airbnb angle is genuinely differentiated:** No US company specifically targets STR hosts as primary ICP. Greece would let you build that positioning from scratch. Partner angle: property management companies, real estate agencies selling investment properties.

7. **The biggest Greek-specific risk:** Cultural resistance to "paying for something before it's broken." Greek homeownership culture is high (73%+) but professional maintenance penetration is very low. Education + trust-building is a longer sales cycle than the US.

8. **Start hyperlocal:** One neighborhood in Athens (Kifissia, Glyfada, Kolonaki — wealthy, time-poor). Build density. Don't attempt Thessaloniki in year 1.
